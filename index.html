Bipolar Disorder Stigma

Taha Anwar
SPSY 0828: The Meaning of Madness
December 7, 2026

Bipolar disorder is a mental illness that affects mood, energy, and daily life. The stigma around it is not just a wrong idea. It changes how people are treated, whether they ask for help, and how they see themselves. This page explains what that stigma is, how it harms people, why it is inaccurate, and how awareness can help.

1. What the stigma is

A stigma is usually made of three parts: stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination (Corrigan et al., 2014). A stereotype is a shared negative belief about a person or a group of people. Subsequently, prejudice is agreeing with that negative belief and reacting to it with fear or distaste. Discrimination is acting on this negative belief. For example, someone might exclude someone from an event or social gathering because they have a negative belief about a person due to their race or religion.

With bipolar disorder, a common stereotype is that a person with this disorder is crazy, dangerous, and unable to work or live a normal life. These labels are often applied to a person as a whole and not to a specific episode or symptom that the person may have. In An Unquiet Mind, Kay Redfield Jamison (1995) shows this fear throughout her life. Jamison was a professor and psychiatrist, and she hid her illness at work because she worried that people would judge her if they knew her diagnosis.

2. How it is harmful

The main harm comes from seeking help. Stigma is a major reason as to why people delay treatment or stop it early (Corrigan et al., 2014). People may avoid telling a mental health professional, family, or friends about their symptoms because they do not want to be labeled or degraded. They might also avoid treatment from fear of being viewed as weak or incapable.

Stigma can also become strongly internalized. This means that a person starts to feed into and believe the negative labels about themselves. Livingston and Boyd (2010) reviewed 127 studies and found that internalized stigma was linked to lower self-esteem, lower hope, lower self-efficacy, and lower quality of life. Stigma was also linked to less social support and worse adherence to treatment. Essentially, believing the stigma makes recovery harder.

Jamison (1995) describes a related issue, which is the stress of hiding. Hiding an illness takes a lot of effort and is mentally taxing, and it can leave a person isolated while they are in need of support the most.

3. Why it is inaccurate or misleading

Screen portrayals of mental illness are frequent and mostly negative (Pirkis et al., 2006). Media often focuses on violence, criminality, and unpredictability, while leaving out ordinary daily life. Stuart (2006) found the same pattern where entertainment and news media provide dramatic and distorted images which emphasize dangerousness, criminality, and unpredictability. They also show negative reactions to people with mental illness, including fear, rejection, and ridicule.

These portrayals are very harmful and misleading for three reasons. First, bipolar disorder is episodic, and not a constant cycle. A person can have periods of mania or depression and long periods in between where they can think clearly and work normally. Jamison (1995) had long periods of clear thinking and productive work, and she managed her illness. Second, treatment and recovery are often left out of the story. Stuart (2006) notes that media often skips treatment so viewers do not see that symptoms can be managed. Third, TV shows resolve illnesses far too simply. In many popular shows, a character will undergo bouts of bipolar episodes with little screen time in between. The exaggerated actions of a manic individual are displayed, and then they go back to normal. That suggests a quick fix and not the longer process of living with and managing a mental illness.

4. How awareness can help

Awareness is key and helps immensely when it replaces a stereotype with accurate information and a real story. Corrigan et al. (2014) found that mental health literacy, cultural competence, and family engagement can reduce the negative impact of stigma on seeking care. Programs that share personal stories of recovery and improve support systems can also help greatly.

Stuart (2006) makes a similar point regarding the media. The media can be an ally when it challenges public prejudice, starts civil public debate, and shows positive, human stories about people living with mental illness. Jamison (1995) is a clear example of this, where she writes openly about her own experience and showed that a person with bipolar disorder can be a working professional, an intellectual, and a person with a great life, not only a diagnosis.

Three simple changes help when discussing bipolar disorder:

  • Use accurate language. Describe the symptoms and episodes, and avoid using labels like crazy and insane.
  • Show the full picture. Include treatment, recovery, work, and daily life, not only the moment of crisis.
  • Listen to lived experiences. Real stories counter stereotypes better than an attention-grabbing title or scene.

Reducing stigma won't remove bipolar disorder on its own, but it can remove the barrier to care. When people are less afraid of being judged, they are more likely to ask for help, stay in treatment, and get support from work, family, and friends.

If you need help now

If you or someone you know is in crisis, call or text 988 in the United States to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. It is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.
